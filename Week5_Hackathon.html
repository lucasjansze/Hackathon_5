The challenge

Pick one concrete prediction problem about work, income or economic security, find a real dataset for it yourself, and train three classifiers on it: a k-nearest-neighbours model, a logistic regression, and one model of your own choice (a decision tree, a random forest, gradient boosting, an SVM, naive Bayes, anything scikit-learn offers that you can explain). Tune all three, compare them fairly, and tell a named user which one they should use, or why none of them is good enough.

This hackathon is not about the model. Fitting a model is one line of code. It is about everything around that line: understanding and cleaning the data, choosing the right train and testsets, tuning, and choosing a metric that matches what a wrong prediction costs a real person — someone who does not get the loan, the job interview or the support. A model that scores 0.99 on a leaky split fails this hackathon; a robust model that scores 0.72 honestly, and knows why, passes it.

Rules of the game: you must use this week's tool (scikit-learn, with all three models), your problem must address this week's SDG, and the result must be working and reproducible — a notebook that runs from the raw data file to the final comparison without manual steps. The problem you choose and the dataset you find are entirely up to you. You may use an AI assistant to help you write code, but every choice in the notebook is yours: if you cannot explain why a line is there, it should not be there.

Finding your dataset

Finding and judging a dataset is part of the assignment. Your dataset must be:

  • Real and documented: you can say who collected it, how, when, and from which population, and under which licence you may use it. Many popular datasets on Kaggle.com have no known origin or were generated synthetically. If you cannot find out where the data comes from, pick another dataset.
  • A classification problem: one target column with a small number of classes (yes/no, low/medium/high), tabular, one row per person, household, loan or company.
  • Big enough: at least 300 rows and at least 5 usable features.
  • New: not a dataset built into scikit-learn, and not the breast cancer or the Titanic dataset from this week's lecture.

Good places to start looking for a usuable dataset: the UCI Machine Learning Repository (filter on the Business and Social Science areas, task Classification), OpenML, and the open-data portals of statistics offices and governments (CBS, data.overheid.nl, data.europa.eu). Topics that fit: income and poverty, unemployment and getting back into work, fair pay, credit and debt, small businesses, job security. Examples of the right kind: UCI Adult (Census Income), Bank Marketing, Default of Credit Card Clients, Statlog German Credit. You may use one of these, but you do not have to.

Doing the data science properly — required steps

Your notebook follows these steps, in this order, with a short markdown cell per step saying what you did and why:

  • Frame the problem. What is the target, what is the positive class, who uses the prediction and for what decision? Which mistake is worse for that user — a false negative or a false positive? Choose your main metric (recall, precision, F1, balanced accuracy, ROC-AUC) before you train anything, and say why.
  • Explore the data. Size and types, missing values, class balance, distributions, duplicates, impossible values (an age of 0 or a 168-hour working week is a missing value in disguise), and columns that leak the answer because they are only known after the outcome.
  • Split first. Set aside a test set (for example 20 %, stratified, with a fixed random_state) before you clean, scale or tune anything. You touch it exactly once, at the very end.
  • Pre-process inside a Pipeline. Imputing missing values, scaling (KNN and logistic regression need it — explain why) and encoding categorical columns all happen inside a scikit-learn Pipeline / ColumnTransformer, so they are fitted on training data only. Record every row or column you drop, and why.
  • Set a baseline. A DummyClassifier that always predicts the most common class. Every model has to beat it on your main metric — on unbalanced data that is harder than it sounds.
  • Tune with cross-validation. On the training set only, with the same folds and the same metric for all three models (GridSearchCV or your own loop): at least n_neighbors for KNN, C for logistic regression, and the main hyperparameter(s) of your own model. Show at least one plot or table of the score against a hyperparameter, and report the mean and spread over the folds.
  • Evaluate once on the test set. Per model and for the baseline: a confusion matrix, precision, recall and F1. Put the training score, the cross-validation score and the test score next to each other and say what the gaps tell you about over- or underfitting.
  • Look at the errors. Which cases does your best model get wrong? If the data has a group column (sex, age band, nationality, region), compare performance per group.
  • Recommend. Which model would you hand to your user, and why? The highest score is not automatically the answer — think of the type of errors, explainability and whether the difference is bigger than the spread over the folds. "None of them, because..." is an allowed answer.
  • Use it. End with a cell where a new, made-up case is entered and your recommended model returns its prediction and probability.

Common ways to fail without noticing: scaling or imputing before the split; tuning on the test set; comparing models on different splits or different metrics; reporting accuracy on data where 90 % of the rows are one class; duplicate rows ending up in both train and test; and forgetting random_state, so nobody (including you) gets the same numbers twice.

How the standard rubric applies to this hackathon

The standard hackathon rubric is used, unchanged. This is what each criterion means when the product is a trained and evaluated model:

K1 — AI tool

All three models (KNN, logistic regression, your own choice) are trained, tuned and evaluated with scikit-learn in the submitted notebook. A missing model, or a model that is only fitted with default settings and never evaluated, does not meet this.

K2 — SDG relevance

The model predicts one specific outcome about work, income or economic security for one specific group, not "the economy" in general.

K3 — Scope

The prediction supports a real decision of a named user, and it is evaluated honestly. Three models with default settings and an accuracy score is a technical exercise and does not meet this.

1 — Problem definition

The outcome, the population, the setting and how big the problem is — with a source. Also: does the population in your dataset match the population you want to use the model for?

2 — User group

Who uses the prediction and what they do with it, who is affected by it (the people it makes predictions about), and who is not represented in the data.

3 — Solution description

The pipeline step by step — source, cleaning, split, pre-processing, tuning, evaluation — concretely enough that someone else gets your exact numbers from your notebook and data.

4 — Problem-solution fit

Why your main metric matches the cost of errors for your user, why you recommend this model, and why machine learning beats the simple alternative (the baseline, or a single threshold on one feature).

5 — Working prototype

Restart and Run All works: raw file in, comparison table and a prediction for a new case out, without manual steps. In the presentation you show the comparison and make one live prediction.

6 — Ethical reasoning

Risks specific to this model and this data (see below), their consequences for the people it makes predictions about, and what you did about them.

 

Ethical reflection (required)

Your model decides something about people's livelihoods: who gets the loan, the interview or the extra support. Who is in your dataset — which country, which year, which ages, sexes and backgrounds — and who is missing? Does your model make the same kind of mistakes for men and women, young and old, natives and migrants? Check it for at least one group if your data allows it. If you removed a sensitive column, is it still hiding in another one (a postcode, a job title, the number of years in the country)? What does a false negative cost the person, and what does a false positive cost them? And were the people in the data asked, and does the licence allow what you are doing with it?

Include your answer in your submission — a short paragraph is enough, but it must be honest and specific to your model, and it must say what you did about at least one of the risks (a different metric, a different threshold, a subgroup check, a warning in the README about who the model must not be used for).

What to submit

  1. The notebook (.ipynb), run from top to bottom with its outputs saved, needs to be able to run in google collab without extra installs.
  2. The data: the file in your repo if the licence allows it and it is under 25 MB, otherwise a cell in your notebook that downloads it plus the link
  3. A dataset card/paragraph (in the README): source and link, who collected it, when and how, licence, number of rows and columns, the target and its class balance, and known limitations
  4. The comparison table: per model the best hyperparameters, the cross-validation score (mean ± spread), and the test precision, recall and F1 — with the baseline as the first row
  5. A README: the problem and your source, the user and the decision, which SDG it addresses and why, the recommended model and why, and how to run the notebook (Python packages and versions)
  6. Your ethical reflection (see above)
